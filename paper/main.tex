\section{Introduction}

This paper documents the construction of an Online Portfolio Selection
(OLPS) benchmark suite, following Li and Hoi's \emph{Online Portfolio
Selection: Principles and Algorithms}. The eventual goal is a learned
strategy compared honestly against the book's classical algorithms. Rather
than presenting only a final, cleaned-up methodology, this paper is
organized around how the project actually unfolded: each algorithm was
implemented, run against real data, and in several cases exposed a problem
-- a wrong assumption, a methodological pitfall, a mismatch between the
book's idealized formulas and what a real free data source can provide --
that changed the scope of what came next. Those changes, and the reasoning
behind them, are reported alongside the final design, not hidden behind it.
Section~\ref{sec:formulation} fixes notation. Section~\ref{sec:process}
is the core of the paper: a chronological account of the project's
development. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports where things currently
stand, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} is explicit about what remains
open and why it was left that way.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Consider a market of $m$ assets traded over $n$ periods. At the end of
period $t$, the price relative vector $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^m_{+}$ records,
for each asset $i$, the ratio of its closing price at $t$ to its closing
price at $t-1$:
\[
    x_t = (x_{t,1}, \dots, x_{t,m}), \qquad
    x_{t,i} = \frac{p_{t,i}}{p_{t-1,i}}.
\]

Before observing $x_t$, an online portfolio selection strategy chooses a
portfolio vector $b_t$ lying on the simplex
\[
    \Delta_m = \left\{ b \in \mathbb{R}^m_{+} : \sum_{i=1}^m b_i = 1 \right\},
\]
using only the price relative history $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$ observed so
far. Each entry $b_{t,i}$ is the fraction of current wealth allocated to
asset $i$ for period $t$.

Once $x_t$ is revealed, the portfolio realizes a period return
\[
    s_t = b_t \cdot x_t = \sum_{i=1}^m b_{t,i} x_{t,i},
\]
and cumulative wealth evolves as
\[
    S_t = S_{t-1} \, s_t, \qquad S_0 = 1.
\]

The goal of an OLPS strategy is to choose the sequence $b_1, \dots, b_n$
so as to maximize the final wealth $S_n = \prod_{t=1}^n b_t \cdot x_t$,
without knowledge of future price relatives.

\section{Development Process}
\label{sec:process}

\subsection{Benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

The first working piece was the \texttt{Strategy} interface: every
algorithm implements a single method, \texttt{update(history)}, a pure
function of the price relative history observed so far that returns the
next portfolio $b_t$. This choice -- no strategy holds state between calls
-- was made early and, as Section~\ref{sec:ons} describes, later became a
real constraint the project deliberately chose not to break.

Three benchmarks from the book's Chapter 3 were implemented against this
interface. Buy-and-Hold (BAH) invests once and lets weights drift with the
market: $S_n(\text{BAH}(b_1)) = b_1 \cdot \left(\bigodot_{t=1}^n x_t\right)$.
Constant Rebalanced Portfolios (CRP) actively rebalance back to a fixed
$b$ every period, $S_n(\text{CRP}(b)) = \prod_{t=1}^n b \cdot x_t$. Best
Stock invests all capital, from day one, in whichever single asset turns
out to have the best return \emph{over the entire window}:
$b^0 = \arg\max_{b \in \Delta_m} b \cdot \left(\bigodot_{t=1}^n x_t\right)$.
The book states this outright: Best Stock ``can only be calculated with
complete market sequences'' -- it is a hindsight benchmark by definition,
not a strategy a real trader could execute, and this distinction turned
out to matter later (Section~\ref{sec:beststock}).

These three were first verified end to end against a small, three-ticker
example (Apple, Microsoft, Google) pulled live via \texttt{yfinance}. That
choice of tickers was convenient and, as it turned out, a methodological
mistake in its own right -- see Section~\ref{sec:survivorship}.

\subsection{Follow the Winner}
\label{sec:ftw}

Before adding new algorithms, the data pipeline was refactored: the single
function that had called \texttt{yfinance} directly was split into
\texttt{get\_prices} (the only function that talks to a data backend) and
\texttt{get\_price\_relatives} (built on top of it, unchanged for every
caller). The motivation was forward-looking rather than a problem
encountered yet: the project anticipates eventually moving from
\texttt{yfinance} to a Bloomberg terminal, and wanted that swap to mean
changing one function, not every strategy or notebook. No backend
abstraction beyond a single function was built, since a second backend did
not exist yet to design against.

Three Follow-the-Winner strategies from Chapter 4 were then implemented,
in order of increasing complexity: Universal Portfolios (UP), Exponential
Gradient (EG), and Online Newton Step (ONS).

\paragraph{Universal Portfolios (UP).}
Cover's UP (Cover, 1991) treats every constant rebalanced portfolio
$b \in \Delta_m$ as a fund manager, and pools their wealth under a prior
$\mu$ over the simplex:
\[
    b_{t+1} = \frac{\int_{\Delta_m} b \, S_t(b) \, d\mu(b)}
                   {\int_{\Delta_m} S_t(b) \, d\mu(b)}, \qquad
    S_t(b) = \prod_{\tau=1}^t b \cdot x_\tau.
\]
With a uniform $\mu$ this integral has no closed form and is exponential
to evaluate exactly. The book's own practical answer is Kalai and
Vempala's (2002) fast randomized simplex walk, and the book even reports
the specific parameters it used for that algorithm
($\delta_0 = 0.004$, $\delta = 0.005$, 100 samples, 500 steps). We
considered implementing that, and decided against it: it is a separate
engineering solution for tractability at scale, not part of UP's actual
mathematical definition, and would have added a nontrivial random-walk
sampler to audit for a thesis reference implementation whose priority was
staying legible and traceable back to the book's own formula. Instead we
implemented a direct Monte Carlo approximation -- $K$ portfolios drawn
once, uniformly from $\Delta_m$, wealth-weighted and averaged every period
-- a literal discretization of the definition above rather than a faster
but more opaque algorithm for the same thing.

\paragraph{Exponential Gradient (EG).}
EG (Helmbold, Schapire, Singer \& Warmuth, 1998) takes a multiplicative
step toward the assets that performed well last period:
\[
    b_{t+1,i} = \frac{b_{t,i} \exp\!\left(\eta \, x_{t,i} / (b_t \cdot x_t)\right)}
                     {\sum_{j=1}^m b_{t,j} \exp\!\left(\eta \, x_{t,j} / (b_t \cdot x_t)\right)}.
\]
As $\eta \to 0$ this degenerates to the uniform constant rebalanced
portfolio, which was used as a direct sanity check on the implementation
before trusting it on real data. We use $\eta = 0.05$, the value the book
itself cites as empirically best.

\paragraph{Online Newton Step (ONS).}
\label{sec:ons}
ONS (Agarwal, Hazan, Kale \& Schapire, 2006) solves the
Follow-the-Regularized-Leader objective with a Newton step built from the
curvature of accumulated log-wealth, then projects back onto the simplex
under that curvature's own norm:
\begin{align*}
    A_t &= \sum_{\tau=1}^t \frac{x_\tau x_\tau^\top}{(b_\tau \cdot x_\tau)^2} + I, \\
    c_t &= \left(1 + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) \sum_{\tau=1}^t \frac{x_\tau}{b_\tau \cdot x_\tau}, \\
    b_{t+1} &= \Pi_{\Delta_m}^{A_t}\!\left(\delta \, A_t^{-1} c_t\right).
\end{align*}
This is where two real implementation decisions came up, not just a
formula to transcribe.

The first was architectural. $A_t$ and $c_t$ are naturally described as
running sums, updated incrementally as each period arrives -- and the
book narrates ONS as an inherently online, stateful algorithm. The
project's own existing strategies, however, were all pure functions of
\texttt{history} with no memory between calls (Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}),
a contract stated explicitly in the codebase's own documentation. Making
ONS stateful would have introduced a pattern nothing else in the codebase
used, and would silently assume \texttt{update()} is always called exactly
once per period, in strictly increasing order -- true of how the backtest
engine happens to call strategies today, but not a guarantee anything
enforces. We chose to keep ONS stateless too: it replays its entire
recursion -- rebuilding $A_t$, $c_t$, and every past portfolio
$b_1, \dots, b_t$ -- from the given history on every single call, rather
than caching anything. The cost is real: roughly $O(n^2)$ quadratic-program
solves over a full backtest of $n$ periods instead of $O(n)$. At this
project's current scale (a few hundred periods, on the order of thirty
assets) this cost was measured directly rather than assumed: a single
\texttt{update()} call at 29 assets and 20 periods of history took under
0.3 seconds, and a full end-to-end backtest with ONS took roughly five to
seven minutes, dominated entirely by these solves. That is acceptable for
now; it would need revisiting before ONS could be run on a much longer
history or a much larger asset universe.

The second was the projection itself. $\Pi_{\Delta_m}^{A_t}$ is a
Mahalanobis-type projection under $A_t$, not a plain Euclidean one, so it
is a genuine small quadratic program at every period, not a closed-form
expression. Rather than hand-rolling a QP solver -- exactly the kind of
intricate, hard-to-audit code this project otherwise avoids -- it is
solved with \texttt{cvxpy}, added as a dependency for this purpose.

Parameters: $\beta = 1$. The book states what is arguably the same
step-size constant in two different places with two different symbols --
Chapter 4's $\delta$ and Chapter 12's $\gamma = 1/8$, both attributed to
Agarwal et al. (2006) -- without reconciling them explicitly. We treat
$\delta = 1/8$ as our own interpretive choice, not a direct quote from
either source.

\subsection{The Survivorship Bias Problem}
\label{sec:survivorship}

With all six benchmark and Follow-the-Winner strategies working, the
three-ticker universe used since Section~\ref{sec:process}
(Apple, Microsoft, Google) was revisited directly: it is three of today's
largest, most successful companies, chosen for convenience with the full
benefit of hindsight about how they turned out. This is the textbook
survivorship bias mistake the OLPS literature warns about -- building a
backtest from stocks known today to have done well, rather than the actual
universe of stocks that existed at the time, including ones that later
underperformed, were delisted, or went bankrupt.

A fully rigorous fix needs two things a free data source may not have:
point-in-time index membership (what was actually in an index on a past
date, not today's list) and price history for securities that no longer
trade. \texttt{yfinance} provides neither -- it only serves data for
currently-listed tickers. We confirmed this is exactly the kind of
capability a Bloomberg terminal is built to provide, which the project
plans to migrate to eventually (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); until
then, the fix had to work within \texttt{yfinance}'s limits rather than
around them.

The mitigation adopted was a rule-based, point-in-time ticker universe
instead of a hand-picked one: the 30 constituents of the Dow Jones
Industrial Average as of the backtest's start date, 2023-01-01,
reconstructed from the index's known reconstitution history (its
membership was unchanged from 2020-08-31 until 2024-02-26, so this list is
exact for the window backtested here) rather than today's constituents.
This is not a complete fix -- it is one fixed snapshot rather than a
membership history tracked through the whole window (no changes happened
to occur here, but none were tracked either), and it inherits whatever
survivorship bias already exists in how Dow membership itself evolves.

Attempting to fetch all 30 surfaced a direct, live example of the problem
it was meant to mitigate: Walgreens Boots Alliance (WBA) was a genuine Dow
constituent throughout 2023, but was taken private in 2025, and
\texttt{yfinance} now serves \emph{no} historical data for it at all, not
even for the period while it was actively trading. Rather than treating
this as a bug to route around, it was left out and documented as exactly
what it is: evidence that a data source tracking only currently-listed
securities makes it structurally difficult to include anything that did
not survive to the present, even when the explicit intent is to avoid
selecting on survival. The final universe used has 29 assets.

\subsection{Visualization Iteration}
\label{sec:viz}

The end-to-end script's wealth-curve plot went through several rounds of
revision driven directly by not being able to read it. The original
version plotted wealth against a bare period index; it was changed to use
the actual trading dates once the universe grew past a toy example.
Separately, with six strategies on one axis, several were close enough in
performance that color alone did not distinguish them, so each strategy
was given a distinct linestyle as well.

Splitting further, once the ticker universe grew to 29 assets, Best
Stock's hindsight-driven wealth pulled far enough ahead that it compressed
the other five strategies -- which perform close to each other -- into an
indistinguishable band at the bottom of the same axes. The first fix was
a second, zoomed panel that excluded Best Stock so the close performers
could actually be compared.

\label{sec:beststock}
That fix was itself reconsidered once Best Stock's nature was examined
more carefully. As Section~\ref{sec:process} notes, and as the book states
directly, Best Stock is defined using the entire backtest window in
advance -- it is not a strategy that makes decisions causally the way
BAH, CRP, UP, EG, and ONS do, all of which are verified (via how the
backtest engine calls them) to see only $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$ before
producing $b_t$. Since Best Stock cannot be made realistic without
changing what it is -- that would stop it from being the hindsight ceiling
the book defines it as -- it was removed from the plot entirely rather
than kept as a labeled reference. It is still computed and printed to the
console for context, but the graph now shows only strategies that decide
under the same causal constraint.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Throughout all of the above, strategies could only be compared by
eyeballing final wealth and wealth curves. The book itself defines a
standard evaluation toolkit in Chapter 2.3 -- annualized percentage yield
(APY), annualized volatility, Sharpe ratio, drawdown and maximum drawdown,
Calmar ratio, and a Student's $t$-test for whether observed profitability
could be explained by chance -- which had never been implemented, despite
predating even the Benchmarks chapter. This gap was identified as the
highest-value next step precisely because closing it required no new
algorithmic content.

Existing libraries (\texttt{empyrical}, \texttt{quantstats}, \texttt{ffn})
implement equivalent metrics and were considered as a shortcut. We chose
to hand-roll every formula directly from the book instead, for the same
reason UP, EG, and ONS were: a library's own conventions for annualization,
degrees of freedom, or the exact construction of a ratio are not guaranteed
to match the book's specific definitions, and confirming that they do
would mean reading the library's source anyway. Every formula in this
project is kept traceable line-by-line back to the book rather than
delegated to a dependency whose internals are trusted on faith. Each metric
was checked by hand against a small synthetic wealth series before being
trusted on real data.

\subsection{Follow the Loser}
\label{sec:ftl}

The next block of the book's Part II principles, Chapter 5, is the
mean-reversion (``follow the loser'') counterpart to Follow the Winner:
rather than betting on trends continuing, these strategies transfer
wealth from a period's outperformers to its underperformers. The chapter
itself defines only one concrete algorithm, Anticorrelation (Anticor);
the book's own machine-learning contributions built on the same
mean-reversion principle -- Passive Aggressive Mean Reversion (PAMR),
Confidence Weighted Mean Reversion (CWMR), and Online Moving Average
Reversion (OLMAR) -- live in Part III, three chapters later. All four
were implemented together, mirroring how Follow the Winner already
spanned multiple chapters rather than a single one.

\paragraph{Anticorrelation (Anticor).}
Borodin, El-Yaniv \& Gogan's Anticor (2004, book \S5.2) compares two
adjacent windows of $w$ periods of log price relatives, and claims a
wealth transfer from asset $i$ to asset $j$ whenever $i$ outperformed
$j$ in the more recent window and the two are positively
cross-correlated, sized by that correlation net of each asset's own
negative autocorrelation. Unlike the other three strategies, Anticor's
claim-and-transfer mechanism keeps the portfolio on the simplex by
construction -- weight only ever moves between existing entries of
$b_t$ -- so no separate projection step is needed. The book does not
state a default window size; we chose $w=5$, matching PAMR/OLMAR's own
book-cited windows, and note this explicitly as our own choice rather
than a quote.

\paragraph{Passive Aggressive Mean Reversion (PAMR).}
PAMR (Li, Hoi \& Gopalkrishnan, 2012, book Ch.~9) defines an
$\epsilon$-insensitive loss on the realized portfolio return,
$\ell_\epsilon(b_t; x_t) = \max(0, b_t \cdot x_t - \epsilon)$, and takes
a closed-form passive-aggressive step toward the underperforming
assets:
\[
    b_{t+1} = \Pi_{\Delta_m}\!\left(b_t - \tau_t (x_t - \bar{x}_t \mathbf{1})\right),
    \qquad
    \tau_t = \frac{\ell_\epsilon(b_t; x_t)}{\lVert x_t - \bar{x}_t \mathbf{1} \rVert^2},
\]
where $\bar{x}_t$ is the mean price relative across assets and
$\Pi_{\Delta_m}$ is a plain Euclidean projection onto the simplex.
$\epsilon = 0.5$ is the book's own real-market default (Sec.~13.3.2);
an earlier attempt used $\epsilon = 1$, chosen because it exactly
reproduces the book's own worked example (Table 9.1) step by step --
but the book itself notes $\epsilon \geq 1$ degrades PAMR toward
uniform CRP, and $\epsilon = 1$ turned out to be far too passive on
real data (Section~\ref{sec:ftl-verify}).

\paragraph{Confidence Weighted Mean Reversion (CWMR).}
CWMR (Li, Hoi, Zhao \& Gopalkrishnan, 2013, book Ch.~10) is the most
involved of the four: $b$ is modeled as a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu,
\Sigma)$ with diagonal $\Sigma$, so each asset's weight carries its own
confidence (a smaller variance meaning more confidence). Each period
solves a scalar Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_{t+1}$ from a quadratic
whose coefficients are given only in the book's Appendix B.3.1, not the
main chapter text -- getting this wrong silently would have produced a
strategy that runs without error but computes something other than
CWMR, so the appendix was read directly rather than reconstructed from
the chapter's higher-level description. We implement the CWMR-Var
variant (the book's first, simpler linearization) with $\phi = 1.64$
($\approx 90\%$ confidence) and $\epsilon = 0.5$, both the book's own
real-market defaults (Sec.~13.3.3).

\paragraph{Online Moving Average Reversion (OLMAR).}
OLMAR (Li \& Hoi, 2012, book Ch.~11) targets multi-period rather than
single-period reversion: it predicts the next price relative from a
simple moving average of the last $w-1$ observed relatives,
\[
    \hat{x}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{w}\left(1 + \frac{1}{x_t} + \frac{1}{x_t x_{t-1}}
    + \cdots + \frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{w-2} x_{t-i}}\right),
\]
then runs the same passive-aggressive step as PAMR against the
\emph{predicted} relative rather than the realized one. We use the
book's own real-market reference settings, $w=5$ and $\epsilon=10$
(Sec.~13.3.4, Figs.~13.9-13.10) -- not the $\epsilon=2$ used in the
book's own Table 11.2, which is illustrative for that section's small
two-asset toy markets specifically, a distinction that mattered in
practice (Section~\ref{sec:ftl-verify}).

\paragraph{A shared projection, and why it avoids \texttt{cvxpy}.}
PAMR, CWMR, and OLMAR all need the same Euclidean projection onto the
simplex after their unconstrained update step -- the same constraint
ONS's own projection solves (Section~\ref{sec:ons}), but under the
identity metric rather than ONS's curvature matrix $A_t$. Reusing
ONS's \texttt{cvxpy}-based approach was the first instinct, but ONS's
own stateless-replay design (Section~\ref{sec:ons}) already costs
$O(n^2)$ solves over a backtest of $n$ periods; adding three more
strategies with the same replay contract and a \texttt{cvxpy} call
inside that $O(n^2)$ loop would have made a multi-year backtest
impractical. The book itself cites a closed-form, sort-based simplex
projection for exactly this case (Duchi et al., 2008), which is what
PAMR/CWMR/OLMAR use instead -- an $O(m \log m)$ formula, not a general
quadratic program, extracted once into a shared
\texttt{strategies/simplex.py} used by all three.

\paragraph{Verification.}
\label{sec:ftl-verify}
With no automated test suite (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), each
strategy was checked the same way the project has checked every
algorithm so far: against small examples the book works out by hand.
PAMR against the book's Table 9.1 and CWMR against its Table 10.2 both
reproduce the book's own portfolio sequence exactly, period by period,
on the two-asset markets those tables describe. This is also how the
$\epsilon=1$ (PAMR) and $\epsilon=2$ (OLMAR) parameter choices were
first tried and then corrected: both values were the ones that
reproduce a book worked example exactly, which is a reasonable thing to
optimize for on a toy table but turned out to be the wrong thing to
optimize for on real data, where the book's own separately-stated
empirical defaults ($\epsilon=0.5$ for PAMR, $\epsilon=10$ for OLMAR)
are what it actually recommends. Anticor was checked differently, since
the book gives no worked numeric table for it: on a synthetic market
with one asset trending up and the other flat, it correctly transfers
weight to the laggard; on the book's own alternating two-asset example
(Table 5.1) it makes no transfer at all, which is also correct -- that
market is perfectly \emph{negatively} cross-correlated between the two
windows Anticor compares, and Anticor's mechanism requires positive
cross-correlation to trigger, a real edge case rather than a bug.

\subsection{Revisiting Survivorship Bias: Tracking Reconstitution}
\label{sec:survivorship2}

Section~\ref{sec:survivorship}'s point-in-time snapshot fixed the
``chose today's winners'' mistake but, by its own admission, not the
deeper one: it is a single fixed ticker list, valid only because the
specific year backtested (2023) happened to fall inside a stretch where
Dow membership never changed. Revisiting it surfaced a second, related
problem: 2023 was also a strong bull year, so Buy-and-Hold already wins
outright (Table~\ref{tab:results-2023}) and there is no fluctuating
market for a mean-reversion strategy to exploit -- the same condition
the book's own Table 5.1 uses to motivate Follow the Loser in the first
place. Both problems pointed at the same fix: a backtest window where
the market actually moved enough to test mean reversion is also a
window more likely to have seen a real Dow reconstitution, since both
are symptoms of the same thing -- the 2023 window was simply an
unusually quiet year on both counts.

The financial crisis, 2007-06-01 to 2009-12-31, was chosen on exactly
that reasoning: a genuine crash and partial recovery, and a period
during which the Dow's membership changed three times, each verified
against Wikipedia's ``List of changes to the Dow Jones Industrial
Average'' and contemporary news coverage rather than recalled from
memory alone. Altria and Honeywell were replaced by Bank of America and
Chevron (2008-02-19); AIG was replaced by Kraft Foods (2008-09-22); and
General Motors and Citigroup were replaced by Cisco and Travelers
(2009-06-08), the last change made, in Dow Jones's own words at the
time, because GM and Citigroup were ``in the midst of substantial
restructuring.''

Tracking this properly needed two things Section~\ref{sec:survivorship}
did not: a membership \emph{timeline} rather than one snapshot
(\texttt{universes/djia\_universe.py}, a literal table of the three reconstitution
dates above), and a way to run a backtest whose asset universe changes
mid-window. The existing \texttt{Strategy} interface already resets
\texttt{history} to empty at the start of any single \texttt{update()}
replay (Section~\ref{sec:ftw}), so the natural fix was not to make
\texttt{n\_assets} itself dynamic, but to give each reconstitution
segment its own fresh strategy instance -- correct, not a workaround,
since asset columns mean different tickers before and after a
reconstitution even when the count stays the same. A new orchestrator,
\texttt{engine.run\_backtest\_segmented}, runs the existing
\texttt{run\_backtest} once per segment and chains wealth across
segment boundaries by rebasing each segment's own $S_0 = 1$ onto the
previous segment's final wealth.

Building the 30-ticker roster as of 2007-06-01 surfaced, and confirmed
directly against \texttt{yfinance} rather than assumed, two further
gaps of exactly the kind Section~\ref{sec:survivorship} documented for
Walgreens Boots Alliance. General Motors filed Chapter 11 in June 2009;
its pre-bankruptcy equity was cancelled in the reorganization (by the
time the Dow itself dropped it, the index listed the entity as
``Motors Liquidation Company,'' not GM), and \texttt{yfinance} serves no
historical data for it at all for the period it was still trading.
Kraft Foods Inc. has no single successor after its 2012 split into
Mondelez and Kraft Foods Group, and its ticker, \texttt{KFT}, is
likewise unavailable. Both are dropped from every segment in which they
would otherwise appear, the same treatment WBA received, and for the
same reason: evidence of the data source's blind spot toward securities
that did not survive to the present, not a bug to route around. A third
ticker, United Technologies (\texttt{UTX}), looked like the same
problem at first -- \texttt{yfinance} has no data under that symbol
either -- but turned out not to be one: the company is still trading,
merged into Raytheon Technologies in 2020, and its continuous price
history (including the 2007-2009 period) is simply filed under the new
ticker, \texttt{RTX}. Fetching it under its current symbol while
displaying it as \texttt{UTX} is a rename, not a survivorship gap, and
the two were kept explicitly distinct in \texttt{universes/djia\_universe.py}
rather than folded into the same exclusion list.

The resulting universe has 29 assets in three of the four segments and
28 in the third (2008-09-22 to 2009-06-08, where both GM and KFT are
simultaneously absent).

\subsection{Broadening the Window Further: 26 Years, Not One Regime}
\label{sec:broaden}

The 2007-2009 results (Section~\ref{sec:results}) were unsettling enough
to warrant a direct check before being trusted: PAMR and CWMR losing
97\% looked less like a market behaving badly and more like something
that could be a bug. Two checks addressed this directly rather than by
assumption. First, CWMR's closed-form update was re-derived from the
book's Appendix B (Equation B.12) term by term against the
implementation; every coefficient matched exactly. Second, PAMR's
actual day-by-day portfolio during the September-October 2008 crash was
traced directly: on 2008-09-25 it placed 100\% of the portfolio in
Citigroup, betting on reversion after Citigroup had the worst return
the prior day -- and that specific bet won, Citigroup gained 2.4\% the
next day. Days later, the same all-in logic repeated on Citigroup again
and lost 5\%. This is PAMR doing exactly what it is specified to do,
not a bug -- and it is close to the book's own illustration (Section~9.4)
of why an uncapped PAMR can be dangerous.

That ruled out a bug, but not a second concern: the 2007-2009 window
was itself chosen deliberately, for being a crisis -- the same category
of bias (not generically wrong, but a specific regime picked for a
specific reason) as the original three-ticker 2023 snapshot
(Section~\ref{sec:survivorship}), just picked to show drama rather than
convenience. A quick check on the original, independent 2023 window
reinforced this: on that window, instead of collapsing, OLMAR and
Anticor were the two \emph{best}-performing strategies of the entire
set, beating even ONS. The same code produced opposite rankings on two
different real markets, which is what correct code responding honestly
to different data looks like, not a bug -- but it also meant neither
window alone was a fair test.

The fix was to stop picking windows at all: extend
\texttt{universes/djia\_universe.py}'s reconstitution timeline back to
2000-01-01, as far as a verified, point-in-time Dow membership history
reasonably extends, and run one continuous backtest through to the
present (2026-09-01) rather than a window chosen to showcase any one
regime. This spans the dot-com bust, the 2008 crisis, the 2009-2019
bull run, the COVID crash, and the recent AI-driven run -- whatever
actually happened, not a selected slice of it. Fourteen reconstitution
events were added (up from three), each verified against Wikipedia's
``List of changes to the Dow Jones Industrial Average'' and, for the
post-2012 changes, contemporary reporting and S\&P Dow Jones Indices'
own press releases directly (including the most recent change, Alphabet
replacing Verizon on 2026-06-29).

Three further data gaps turned up, each confirmed directly against
\texttt{yfinance} and each a distinct failure mode from the three
already documented (WBA, GM, KFT): DowDuPont (existed only 2017-2019
before splitting three ways into Dow Inc., DuPont, and Corteva, with no
single successor carrying its trading history); Eastman Kodak (its
pre-2004 Dow-era history is unavailable under its old ticker,
independent of and years before its unrelated 2012 bankruptcy); and SBC
Communications, the most interesting case -- SBC itself is a permanent
data gap, but not because the company disappeared. SBC acquired the
original AT\&T Corp in 2005 and renamed itself AT\&T Inc., adopting
ticker \texttt{T} -- a company that, under that new name, is still
trading today. The obvious fix, aliasing SBC's fetch to \texttt{T}
(the same approach already used for United Technologies' 2020 rename to
Raytheon Technologies), was rejected: ticker \texttt{T} already meant a
different, unrelated company (the original AT\&T Corp) for the first
half of SBC's own Dow tenure, so applying that alias uniformly would
silently misattribute AT\&T Corp's returns to SBC's seat for 2000-2005.
SBC is therefore treated as unavailable for its full tenure, not
because nothing survived, but because what survived did so under a
ticker that was already spoken for during part of the period in
question.

Running the backtest at this scale also surfaced a latent correctness
bug, independent of anything above: stress-testing the strategies
against random synthetic data (rather than real market data or the
book's worked examples) made \texttt{ons.py}'s \texttt{cvxpy}-based
projection return portfolio weights with tiny negative entries
($\sim\!10^{-9}$), a known behavior of interior-point solvers operating
near a constraint boundary. This had not appeared in any real backtest
run so far, but would eventually, given enough periods; it is fixed
with a clip (\texttt{np.maximum(b.value, 0.0)}) rather than left to be
found the hard way. Separately, ONS's own $O(n^2)$ solver-call cost
(Section~\ref{sec:ons}), measured at a few minutes over $n\!\sim\!650$
periods, was benchmarked directly at this new scale ($n\!\sim\!6700$)
rather than assumed: an estimated 50+ hours, dominated entirely by
\texttt{cvxpy} overhead repeated quadratically many times. Every other
strategy here is plain \texttt{numpy} with no solver calls and finishes
in minutes regardless of scale. Rather than force ONS through
regardless, or quietly drop it, it is excluded from this window by a
named, documented configuration flag (\texttt{INCLUDE\_ONS} in
\texttt{run.py}) rather than a silent omission.

The resulting point-in-time universe has 30 assets in most segments,
dropping to 27-29 whenever one or more of GM, KFT, WBA, DWDP, EK, or SBC
would otherwise be present.

\subsection{Tuning PAMR's Aggressiveness Parameter}
\label{sec:pamr-tuning}

Plain PAMR's failure mode, traced directly in
Section~\ref{sec:broaden}, is specifically that it is unconstrained: a
single day's loss can drive the portfolio entirely into one asset, with
nothing to prevent it. The book's own fix for exactly this problem is
PAMR-1 and PAMR-2 (book Eqs.~9.3-9.4, 9.7-9.8), which cap or soften the
step size $\tau_t$ via an aggressiveness parameter $C$:
\[
    \tau_t^{\text{PAMR-1}} = \min\!\left(C, \frac{\ell_\epsilon}{\lVert x_t - \bar{x}_t \mathbf{1} \rVert^2}\right),
    \qquad
    \tau_t^{\text{PAMR-2}} = \frac{\ell_\epsilon}{\lVert x_t - \bar{x}_t \mathbf{1} \rVert^2 + \frac{1}{2C}}.
\]
Both were implemented as thin subclasses of \texttt{PAMR}, overriding
only this step-size computation, and both were verified exactly against
the book's own worked numerical example (Sec.~9.4: $x_t=(1.00,0.01)$,
$b_t=(1,0)$, $\epsilon=0.30$, $C=1.00$), which gives, for plain PAMR,
PAMR-1, and PAMR-2 respectively:
\[
    \tau_t = 1.43 / 1.00 / 0.71, \qquad
    b_{t+1} = (0.29,0.71) / (0.50,0.50) / (0.65,0.35).
\]
All three reproduced to the stated precision.

The first test used $C=500$, the midpoint of the book's own tested
sensitivity range (50 to 5000, Fig.~13.6), over which it reports
performance as insensitive to $C$. On this project's data, that held:
PAMR-1/PAMR-2 at $C=500$ performed almost identically to plain,
uncapped PAMR -- still a near-total collapse. Sweeping $C$ across
several orders of magnitude on the 2007-2009 window found why: real
daily price relatives rarely produce an uncapped $\tau_t$ anywhere near
even 50, so a cap of 500 essentially never binds. Well below the book's
tested range, a sharp transition appeared instead -- around $C=0.1$,
performance flips from catastrophic ($-46\%$ APY at $C=500$) to
genuinely profitable ($+5.7\%$ APY at $C=0.1$).

This was flagged as a methodological risk before being trusted: $C=0.1$
was the single best-performing value out of eleven tested, found by
searching on the exact window then being evaluated -- in-sample
overfitting, the classic backtesting trap, not evidence the effect
generalizes. The check was to re-run the same sweep on the much larger,
mostly non-overlapping 2000-2026 window from
Section~\ref{sec:broaden}, which $C=0.1$ was not tuned on. The effect
held, and was if anything stronger there ($C=0.1$: $+11.83\%$ APY,
$t=3.11$, $p=0.001$) -- evidence of a real, generalizing effect rather
than noise-fitting to one crisis window. Table~\ref{tab:pamr-sweep}
reports the comparison.

Rather than report $C=0.1$ -- the specific value a sweep on this
project's own data happened to score best -- the final choice is
$C=1.0$: the book's own worked-example value, justified independently
of any search on this data, not the output of one. It captures nearly
all of the benefit ($+10.04\%$/$+11.05\%$ APY for PAMR-1/PAMR-2 on the
full window, both significant at $p<0.01$) without inviting the
``did you just pick the best number you found'' objection $C=0.1$
would. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports only PAMR-2 at $C=1.0$ going
forward (labeled \texttt{PAMR}) rather than plain PAMR, PAMR-1, and
PAMR-2 side by side -- all three are implemented and this comparison
remains the record of why, but showing three near-duplicate collapsing
or recovering lines in the headline comparison would obscure the one
result that matters: the book's own fix, at the book's own stated
value, took the worst-performing strategy in the entire lineup to the
best one.

No equivalent capped variant exists for CWMR -- its own two variants,
CWMR-Var and CWMR-Stdev, differ in how exactly the same optimization
problem is solved (an approximation versus an exact one), not in how
aggressively either trades, so neither addresses the unconstrained,
single-period concentration that CWMR shares with plain PAMR. CWMR is
therefore left at its uncapped result; this asymmetry -- one algorithm
in the family has a documented fix, the other does not -- is itself
worth noting rather than papered over.

charitably, this is the single-period mean reversion assumption failing

efficiently-priced blue-chip stocks are a harder market for
next-period reversion bets than the book's other four benchmark
datasets, and the failure mode (PAMR and CWMR concentrating aggressively
into whatever looks most oversold each single day, in a market that
kept falling for months) is visible directly in the wealth-curve plot
as a near-total collapse by late 2008 that never recovers, while
Anticor's heuristic, claim-based transfers and OLMAR's moving-average
smoothing both react more slowly and lose far less.

The ranking changes again, and tells a more complete story than either
narrower window did alone. BAH, CRP, UP, and EG still cluster tightly,
now all solidly profitable (APY 9.1-9.9\%, each significant at
$p \leq 0.002$) -- a long enough window for a diversified, causally
correct strategy to show a real edge over cash, consistent with the
historical performance of a broad blue-chip basket. Anticor is
profitable and significant ($p=0.016$) but trails the simple baselines
here, the opposite of its relative standing in the crisis window alone.
CWMR remains a near-total loss, unresolved by anything tried
(Section~\ref{sec:pamr-tuning}). Both OLMAR variants lose money over
the full window -- OLMAR2 (the exponential-moving-average variant) is
worse than OLMAR (-6.01\% vs. -1.09\% APY), not better, so trying the
book's second variant did not rescue it the way capping rescued PAMR;
this asymmetry (one fix works cleanly, the other alternative tried does
not) is as informative as a result that worked.

PAMR is the standout: at $C=1$, the strategy that lost 98\% of its
value uncapped grows to $16.17\times$ over 26.7 years -- the best
final wealth of every causal strategy tested, ahead of CRP, UP, and EG,
with the second-tightest significance in the table ($p=0.005$) after
Best Stock itself. Section~\ref{sec:pamr-tuning} is the full account of
how that number was reached and why $C=1$ rather than a better-scoring
but untrustworthy $C=0.1$.
\label{sec:limitations}

Several gaps were identified during this work and deliberately left open
rather than addressed immediately, each for a stated reason:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No automated test suite.} Every component so far --
    benchmarks, Follow-the-Winner and Follow-the-Loser strategies, and
    the Chapter 2.3 metrics -- has been verified manually: by
    hand-computed checks against the book's own worked examples on small
    synthetic markets, and by running the full pipeline against real
    data and inspecting the output. This was a deliberate choice to match
    the project's existing verification style rather than introduce a
    testing framework partway through, but it does not scale indefinitely
    as more algorithms are added.
    \item \textbf{Survivorship bias is fixed for one specific window, not
    as a general capability.} Sections~\ref{sec:survivorship2}
    and~\ref{sec:broaden}'s reconstitution timeline is a genuine fix --
    real point-in-time membership, tracked through fourteen actual Dow
    changes across 26.7 years, not one static snapshot -- but
    \texttt{universes/djia\_universe.py} is a hand-built, hand-verified table
    specific to the 2000-2026 range, not a general mechanism that would
    automatically extend itself to a different or later date range. The
    underlying data limitation is also still only worked around, not
    removed: \texttt{yfinance} cannot supply price history for delisted
    or ticker-orphaned securities at all (GM, KFT, WBA, SBC, DWDP, and EK
    are permanent gaps, not ones a wider date range or more careful code
    would recover), and a fully correct fix remains a capability the
    planned Bloomberg terminal migration is expected to provide -- the
    \texttt{get\_prices} indirection in \texttt{data.py} exists
    specifically so that migration touches one function, not every
    strategy or script.
    \item \textbf{ONS does not appear in the 2000-2026 results.} Its
    $O(n^2)$ \texttt{cvxpy}-solver cost (Section~\ref{sec:ons}) is an
    estimated 50+ hours at this window's scale (Section~\ref{sec:broaden}),
    benchmarked directly rather than assumed. It remains implemented and
    is included wherever earlier, shorter windows are reported; a
    documented flag (\texttt{INCLUDE\_ONS}) controls whether it runs on
    the full window, left off by default. Making it tractable at this
    scale would mean revisiting the stateless, no-cached-state design
    that Section~\ref{sec:ons} deliberately chose.
    \item \textbf{A known but unfixed data bug.} \texttt{yfinance} returns
    multi-ticker downloads with columns sorted alphabetically rather than
    in the order tickers were requested. This does not affect any
    backtest result, since every strategy operates on asset columns
    positionally and consistently (and the reconstitution-segmented
    backtest now explicitly reindexes each segment by ticker label rather
    than relying on request order, Section~\ref{sec:survivorship2}), but
    it would silently mislabel any future code that assumes the two
    orders match.
    \item \textbf{Maximum drawdown is absolute, not peak-relative.} The
    Chapter 2.3 metric implemented in Section~\ref{sec:process} is
    $\max_t(\max_{\tau \leq t} S_\tau - S_t)$, an absolute wealth
    drop rather than the conventional $\max_t(1 - S_t / \max_{\tau \leq t}
    S_\tau)$ normalized by the peak. This went unnoticed while every
    strategy's wealth stayed close to 1 (Table~\ref{tab:results-2023}),
    since the two definitions barely differ there, but is visible in
    Table~\ref{tab:results-crisis} and, more severely, in the 26.7-year
    results (Table~\ref{tab:results-full}), where a strategy that grows
    to several times its starting wealth before any drawdown can report
    MDD and Calmar figures that are technically correct under this
    definition but hard to read as a risk measure at that scale. The
    numbers are correct under the definition actually implemented;
    whether to switch to the conventional relative definition is left
    open rather than changed silently alongside unrelated work.
    \item \textbf{Remaining book chapters.} Pattern Matching,
    Meta-Algorithms, and the proposed learned strategy in this paper's
    own title are not yet implemented.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The value of this project so far is not only the algorithms implemented,
but the practical realities that surfaced while translating the book's
idealized formulas into working code against real data: a free data
source's blind spot toward anything that did not survive to the present
under its original ticker (six distinct cases now, each a different
failure mode -- taken private, bankrupted, split three ways, or simply
orphaned by a rename that collided with an unrelated earlier company),
an architectural contract worth preserving even when a textbook
algorithm's own narration pulls against it, formulas the book itself
states with inconsistent notation across chapters, and parameters tuned
to reproduce a textbook's own toy example that turned out to be wrong on
real data compared to the values the book separately recommends. Each of
these changed the scope of the work in a specific, traceable way.

Two results stand out as validation of the project's overall approach,
for complementary reasons. The 2000-2026 results
(Section~\ref{sec:broaden}) reproduce, on an independently built window
the book never tested, the same qualitative ranking among mean-reversion
strategies that the book itself reports for this asset class --
evidence the implementations are capturing something real about these
algorithms, not an artifact of one convenient dataset. The PAMR tuning
investigation (Section~\ref{sec:pamr-tuning}) goes a step further: it is
not just a result that matches the book, but a demonstration of a
specific empirical trap and its fix -- an uncapped algorithm failing
catastrophically, a naive parameter search finding a value that fixes
it but cannot be trusted on the window that found it, and a principled
resolution (the book's own stated parameter, not a searched one) that
turns the worst-performing strategy in the entire comparison into the
best-performing one. That process -- including the dead ends, the
overfitting risk caught before being reported, and the mechanistic trace
that ruled out a bug before any of this was trusted -- is reported here
as part of the project's contribution, not smoothed over in favor of a
cleaner-looking final result.
