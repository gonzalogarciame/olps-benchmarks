\section{Problem Formulation}

Consider a market of $m$ assets traded over $n$ periods. At the end of
period $t$, the price relative vector $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^m_{+}$ records,
for each asset $i$, the ratio of its closing price at $t$ to its closing
price at $t-1$:
\[
    x_t = (x_{t,1}, \dots, x_{t,m}), \qquad
    x_{t,i} = \frac{p_{t,i}}{p_{t-1,i}}.
\]

Before observing $x_t$, an online portfolio selection strategy chooses a
portfolio vector $b_t$ lying on the simplex
\[
    \Delta_m = \left\{ b \in \mathbb{R}^m_{+} : \sum_{i=1}^m b_i = 1 \right\},
\]
using only the price relative history $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$ observed so
far. Each entry $b_{t,i}$ is the fraction of current wealth allocated to
asset $i$ for period $t$.

Once $x_t$ is revealed, the portfolio realizes a period return
\[
    s_t = b_t \cdot x_t = \sum_{i=1}^m b_{t,i} x_{t,i},
\]
and cumulative wealth evolves as
\[
    S_t = S_{t-1} \, s_t, \qquad S_0 = 1.
\]

The goal of an OLPS strategy is to choose the sequence $b_1, \dots, b_n$
so as to maximize the final wealth $S_n = \prod_{t=1}^n b_t \cdot x_t$,
without knowledge of future price relatives.

\subsection{Follow the Winner}

Follow-the-Winner strategies push the portfolio toward whichever fixed
allocation has performed best on the data seen so far. We implement three
such strategies, of increasing complexity: Universal Portfolios (UP),
Exponential Gradient (EG), and Online Newton Step (ONS).

\paragraph{Universal Portfolios (UP).}
Cover's UP (Cover, 1991) treats every constant rebalanced portfolio
$b \in \Delta_m$ as a fund manager, and pools their wealth under a prior
$\mu$ over the simplex:
\[
    b_{t+1} = \frac{\int_{\Delta_m} b \, S_t(b) \, d\mu(b)}
                   {\int_{\Delta_m} S_t(b) \, d\mu(b)}, \qquad
    S_t(b) = \prod_{\tau=1}^t b \cdot x_\tau.
\]
With a uniform $\mu$, this integral has no closed form and is exponential
to evaluate exactly. We approximate it by Monte Carlo: $K$ portfolios are
drawn once, uniformly from $\Delta_m$, and $b_{t+1}$ is their wealth-weighted
average at each period. This is a direct discretization of the definition
above, not the fast approximate algorithm (a randomized simplex walk) that
Kalai and Vempala (2002) propose for the same problem at scale;
we chose the simpler Monte Carlo estimate since exact fidelity to the
book's algorithmic definition mattered more here than runtime.

\paragraph{Exponential Gradient (EG).}
EG (Helmbold, Schapire, Singer \& Warmuth, 1998) takes a multiplicative step toward the assets that
performed well last period, with learning rate $\eta$:
\[
    b_{t+1,i} = \frac{b_{t,i} \exp\!\left(\eta \, x_{t,i} / (b_t \cdot x_t)\right)}
                     {\sum_{j=1}^m b_{t,j} \exp\!\left(\eta \, x_{t,j} / (b_t \cdot x_t)\right)}.
\]
As $\eta \to 0$ this degenerates to the uniform constant rebalanced
portfolio, which we use as a sanity check on the implementation. We use
$\eta = 0.05$, the value reported as empirically best in the original paper.

\paragraph{Online Newton Step (ONS).}
ONS (Agarwal, Hazan, Kale \& Schapire, 2006) solves the Follow-the-Regularized-Leader objective
by taking a Newton step using the curvature of the log-wealth accumulated
so far, then projecting back onto the simplex under that curvature's norm:
\begin{align*}
    A_t &= \sum_{\tau=1}^t \frac{x_\tau x_\tau^\top}{(b_\tau \cdot x_\tau)^2} + I, \\
    c_t &= \left(1 + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) \sum_{\tau=1}^t \frac{x_\tau}{b_\tau \cdot x_\tau}, \\
    b_{t+1} &= \Pi_{\Delta_m}^{A_t}\!\left(\delta \, A_t^{-1} c_t\right).
\end{align*}
We use $\beta = 1$. The Agarwal et al. paper's own recommended parameters
are stated with a different symbol, $\gamma = 1/8$, for what plays the same
role as $\delta$ here; the two are not reconciled explicitly in either
source we drew from, so treating $\delta = 1/8$ is our interpretive choice
rather than a direct quote. The projection $\Pi_{\Delta_m}^{A_t}$ is a
Mahalanobis-type projection under $A_t$, not a Euclidean one, so it is
solved as a small quadratic program (via \texttt{cvxpy}) at every period
rather than in closed form.

\emph{Implementation note.} Every strategy in this codebase, including UP
and EG above, is implemented as a pure function of the price relative
history seen so far -- it holds no state between calls. For ONS this is a
deliberate choice, not an oversight: computing $A_t$ and $c_t$ needs every
past portfolio $b_1, \dots, b_t$, not just the price relatives, and those
portfolios could instead be cached incrementally as the strategy runs.
We chose not to, so that ONS keeps the same "just give me the history"
contract as the rest of the strategies, rather than introducing a strategy
that silently assumes it is only ever called once per period, in strictly
increasing order. The trade-off is computational: recomputing the full
recursion on every call costs $O(n^2)$ quadratic-program solves over a
backtest of $n$ periods instead of $O(n)$. At the scale used in this
thesis (a few hundred periods, single-digit numbers of assets) this is a
few minutes, not a bottleneck; it would need revisiting before ONS could
be run on a much longer history.
