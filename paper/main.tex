\section{Introduction}

This paper documents the construction of an Online Portfolio Selection
(OLPS) benchmark suite, following Li and Hoi's \emph{Online Portfolio
Selection: Principles and Algorithms}. The eventual goal is a learned
strategy compared honestly against the book's classical algorithms. Rather
than presenting only a final, cleaned-up methodology, this paper is
organized around how the project actually unfolded: each algorithm was
implemented, run against real data, and in several cases exposed a problem
-- a wrong assumption, a methodological pitfall, a mismatch between the
book's idealized formulas and what a real free data source can provide --
that changed the scope of what came next. Those changes, and the reasoning
behind them, are reported alongside the final design, not hidden behind it.
Section~\ref{sec:formulation} fixes notation. Section~\ref{sec:process}
is the core of the paper: a chronological account of the project's
development. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports where things currently
stand, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} is explicit about what remains
open and why it was left that way.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Consider a market of $m$ assets traded over $n$ periods. At the end of
period $t$, the price relative vector $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^m_{+}$ records,
for each asset $i$, the ratio of its closing price at $t$ to its closing
price at $t-1$:
\[
    x_t = (x_{t,1}, \dots, x_{t,m}), \qquad
    x_{t,i} = \frac{p_{t,i}}{p_{t-1,i}}.
\]

Before observing $x_t$, an online portfolio selection strategy chooses a
portfolio vector $b_t$ lying on the simplex
\[
    \Delta_m = \left\{ b \in \mathbb{R}^m_{+} : \sum_{i=1}^m b_i = 1 \right\},
\]
using only the price relative history $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$ observed so
far. Each entry $b_{t,i}$ is the fraction of current wealth allocated to
asset $i$ for period $t$.

Once $x_t$ is revealed, the portfolio realizes a period return
\[
    s_t = b_t \cdot x_t = \sum_{i=1}^m b_{t,i} x_{t,i},
\]
and cumulative wealth evolves as
\[
    S_t = S_{t-1} \, s_t, \qquad S_0 = 1.
\]

The goal of an OLPS strategy is to choose the sequence $b_1, \dots, b_n$
so as to maximize the final wealth $S_n = \prod_{t=1}^n b_t \cdot x_t$,
without knowledge of future price relatives.

\section{Development Process}
\label{sec:process}

\subsection{Benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

The first working piece was the \texttt{Strategy} interface: every
algorithm implements a single method, \texttt{update(history)}, a pure
function of the price relative history observed so far that returns the
next portfolio $b_t$. This choice -- no strategy holds state between calls
-- was made early and, as Section~\ref{sec:ons} describes, later became a
real constraint the project deliberately chose not to break.

Three benchmarks from the book's Chapter 3 were implemented against this
interface. Buy-and-Hold (BAH) invests once and lets weights drift with the
market: $S_n(\text{BAH}(b_1)) = b_1 \cdot \left(\bigodot_{t=1}^n x_t\right)$.
Constant Rebalanced Portfolios (CRP) actively rebalance back to a fixed
$b$ every period, $S_n(\text{CRP}(b)) = \prod_{t=1}^n b \cdot x_t$. Best
Stock invests all capital, from day one, in whichever single asset turns
out to have the best return \emph{over the entire window}:
$b^0 = \arg\max_{b \in \Delta_m} b \cdot \left(\bigodot_{t=1}^n x_t\right)$.
The book states this outright: Best Stock ``can only be calculated with
complete market sequences'' -- it is a hindsight benchmark by definition,
not a strategy a real trader could execute, and this distinction turned
out to matter later (Section~\ref{sec:beststock}).

These three were first verified end to end against a small, three-ticker
example (Apple, Microsoft, Google) pulled live via \texttt{yfinance}. That
choice of tickers was convenient and, as it turned out, a methodological
mistake in its own right -- see Section~\ref{sec:survivorship}.

\subsection{Follow the Winner}
\label{sec:ftw}

Before adding new algorithms, the data pipeline was refactored: the single
function that had called \texttt{yfinance} directly was split into
\texttt{get\_prices} (the only function that talks to a data backend) and
\texttt{get\_price\_relatives} (built on top of it, unchanged for every
caller). The motivation was forward-looking rather than a problem
encountered yet: the project anticipates eventually moving from
\texttt{yfinance} to a Bloomberg terminal, and wanted that swap to mean
changing one function, not every strategy or notebook. No backend
abstraction beyond a single function was built, since a second backend did
not exist yet to design against.

Three Follow-the-Winner strategies from Chapter 4 were then implemented,
in order of increasing complexity: Universal Portfolios (UP), Exponential
Gradient (EG), and Online Newton Step (ONS).

\paragraph{Universal Portfolios (UP).}
Cover's UP (Cover, 1991) treats every constant rebalanced portfolio
$b \in \Delta_m$ as a fund manager, and pools their wealth under a prior
$\mu$ over the simplex:
\[
    b_{t+1} = \frac{\int_{\Delta_m} b \, S_t(b) \, d\mu(b)}
                   {\int_{\Delta_m} S_t(b) \, d\mu(b)}, \qquad
    S_t(b) = \prod_{\tau=1}^t b \cdot x_\tau.
\]
With a uniform $\mu$ this integral has no closed form and is exponential
to evaluate exactly. The book's own practical answer is Kalai and
Vempala's (2002) fast randomized simplex walk, and the book even reports
the specific parameters it used for that algorithm
($\delta_0 = 0.004$, $\delta = 0.005$, 100 samples, 500 steps). We
considered implementing that, and decided against it: it is a separate
engineering solution for tractability at scale, not part of UP's actual
mathematical definition, and would have added a nontrivial random-walk
sampler to audit for a thesis reference implementation whose priority was
staying legible and traceable back to the book's own formula. Instead we
implemented a direct Monte Carlo approximation -- $K$ portfolios drawn
once, uniformly from $\Delta_m$, wealth-weighted and averaged every period
-- a literal discretization of the definition above rather than a faster
but more opaque algorithm for the same thing.

\paragraph{Exponential Gradient (EG).}
EG (Helmbold, Schapire, Singer \& Warmuth, 1998) takes a multiplicative
step toward the assets that performed well last period:
\[
    b_{t+1,i} = \frac{b_{t,i} \exp\!\left(\eta \, x_{t,i} / (b_t \cdot x_t)\right)}
                     {\sum_{j=1}^m b_{t,j} \exp\!\left(\eta \, x_{t,j} / (b_t \cdot x_t)\right)}.
\]
As $\eta \to 0$ this degenerates to the uniform constant rebalanced
portfolio, which was used as a direct sanity check on the implementation
before trusting it on real data. We use $\eta = 0.05$, the value the book
itself cites as empirically best.

\paragraph{Online Newton Step (ONS).}
\label{sec:ons}
ONS (Agarwal, Hazan, Kale \& Schapire, 2006) solves the
Follow-the-Regularized-Leader objective with a Newton step built from the
curvature of accumulated log-wealth, then projects back onto the simplex
under that curvature's own norm:
\begin{align*}
    A_t &= \sum_{\tau=1}^t \frac{x_\tau x_\tau^\top}{(b_\tau \cdot x_\tau)^2} + I, \\
    c_t &= \left(1 + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) \sum_{\tau=1}^t \frac{x_\tau}{b_\tau \cdot x_\tau}, \\
    b_{t+1} &= \Pi_{\Delta_m}^{A_t}\!\left(\delta \, A_t^{-1} c_t\right).
\end{align*}
This is where two real implementation decisions came up, not just a
formula to transcribe.

The first was architectural. $A_t$ and $c_t$ are naturally described as
running sums, updated incrementally as each period arrives -- and the
book narrates ONS as an inherently online, stateful algorithm. The
project's own existing strategies, however, were all pure functions of
\texttt{history} with no memory between calls (Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}),
a contract stated explicitly in the codebase's own documentation. Making
ONS stateful would have introduced a pattern nothing else in the codebase
used, and would silently assume \texttt{update()} is always called exactly
once per period, in strictly increasing order -- true of how the backtest
engine happens to call strategies today, but not a guarantee anything
enforces. We chose to keep ONS stateless too: it replays its entire
recursion -- rebuilding $A_t$, $c_t$, and every past portfolio
$b_1, \dots, b_t$ -- from the given history on every single call, rather
than caching anything. The cost is real: roughly $O(n^2)$ quadratic-program
solves over a full backtest of $n$ periods instead of $O(n)$. At this
project's current scale (a few hundred periods, on the order of thirty
assets) this cost was measured directly rather than assumed: a single
\texttt{update()} call at 29 assets and 20 periods of history took under
0.3 seconds, and a full end-to-end backtest with ONS took roughly five to
seven minutes, dominated entirely by these solves. That is acceptable for
now; it would need revisiting before ONS could be run on a much longer
history or a much larger asset universe.

The second was the projection itself. $\Pi_{\Delta_m}^{A_t}$ is a
Mahalanobis-type projection under $A_t$, not a plain Euclidean one, so it
is a genuine small quadratic program at every period, not a closed-form
expression. Rather than hand-rolling a QP solver -- exactly the kind of
intricate, hard-to-audit code this project otherwise avoids -- it is
solved with \texttt{cvxpy}, added as a dependency for this purpose.

Parameters: $\beta = 1$. The book states what is arguably the same
step-size constant in two different places with two different symbols --
Chapter 4's $\delta$ and Chapter 12's $\gamma = 1/8$, both attributed to
Agarwal et al. (2006) -- without reconciling them explicitly. We treat
$\delta = 1/8$ as our own interpretive choice, not a direct quote from
either source.

\subsection{The Survivorship Bias Problem}
\label{sec:survivorship}

With all six benchmark and Follow-the-Winner strategies working, the
three-ticker universe used since Section~\ref{sec:process}
(Apple, Microsoft, Google) was revisited directly: it is three of today's
largest, most successful companies, chosen for convenience with the full
benefit of hindsight about how they turned out. This is the textbook
survivorship bias mistake the OLPS literature warns about -- building a
backtest from stocks known today to have done well, rather than the actual
universe of stocks that existed at the time, including ones that later
underperformed, were delisted, or went bankrupt.

A fully rigorous fix needs two things a free data source may not have:
point-in-time index membership (what was actually in an index on a past
date, not today's list) and price history for securities that no longer
trade. \texttt{yfinance} provides neither -- it only serves data for
currently-listed tickers. We confirmed this is exactly the kind of
capability a Bloomberg terminal is built to provide, which the project
plans to migrate to eventually (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); until
then, the fix had to work within \texttt{yfinance}'s limits rather than
around them.

The mitigation adopted was a rule-based, point-in-time ticker universe
instead of a hand-picked one: the 30 constituents of the Dow Jones
Industrial Average as of the backtest's start date, 2023-01-01,
reconstructed from the index's known reconstitution history (its
membership was unchanged from 2020-08-31 until 2024-02-26, so this list is
exact for the window backtested here) rather than today's constituents.
This is not a complete fix -- it is one fixed snapshot rather than a
membership history tracked through the whole window (no changes happened
to occur here, but none were tracked either), and it inherits whatever
survivorship bias already exists in how Dow membership itself evolves.

Attempting to fetch all 30 surfaced a direct, live example of the problem
it was meant to mitigate: Walgreens Boots Alliance (WBA) was a genuine Dow
constituent throughout 2023, but was taken private in 2025, and
\texttt{yfinance} now serves \emph{no} historical data for it at all, not
even for the period while it was actively trading. Rather than treating
this as a bug to route around, it was left out and documented as exactly
what it is: evidence that a data source tracking only currently-listed
securities makes it structurally difficult to include anything that did
not survive to the present, even when the explicit intent is to avoid
selecting on survival. The final universe used has 29 assets.

\subsection{Visualization Iteration}
\label{sec:viz}

The end-to-end script's wealth-curve plot went through several rounds of
revision driven directly by not being able to read it. The original
version plotted wealth against a bare period index; it was changed to use
the actual trading dates once the universe grew past a toy example.
Separately, with six strategies on one axis, several were close enough in
performance that color alone did not distinguish them, so each strategy
was given a distinct linestyle as well.

Splitting further, once the ticker universe grew to 29 assets, Best
Stock's hindsight-driven wealth pulled far enough ahead that it compressed
the other five strategies -- which perform close to each other -- into an
indistinguishable band at the bottom of the same axes. The first fix was
a second, zoomed panel that excluded Best Stock so the close performers
could actually be compared.

\label{sec:beststock}
That fix was itself reconsidered once Best Stock's nature was examined
more carefully. As Section~\ref{sec:process} notes, and as the book states
directly, Best Stock is defined using the entire backtest window in
advance -- it is not a strategy that makes decisions causally the way
BAH, CRP, UP, EG, and ONS do, all of which are verified (via how the
backtest engine calls them) to see only $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$ before
producing $b_t$. Since Best Stock cannot be made realistic without
changing what it is -- that would stop it from being the hindsight ceiling
the book defines it as -- it was removed from the plot entirely rather
than kept as a labeled reference. It is still computed and printed to the
console for context, but the graph now shows only strategies that decide
under the same causal constraint.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Throughout all of the above, strategies could only be compared by
eyeballing final wealth and wealth curves. The book itself defines a
standard evaluation toolkit in Chapter 2.3 -- annualized percentage yield
(APY), annualized volatility, Sharpe ratio, drawdown and maximum drawdown,
Calmar ratio, and a Student's $t$-test for whether observed profitability
could be explained by chance -- which had never been implemented, despite
predating even the Benchmarks chapter. This gap was identified as the
highest-value next step precisely because closing it required no new
algorithmic content.

Existing libraries (\texttt{empyrical}, \texttt{quantstats}, \texttt{ffn})
implement equivalent metrics and were considered as a shortcut. We chose
to hand-roll every formula directly from the book instead, for the same
reason UP, EG, and ONS were: a library's own conventions for annualization,
degrees of freedom, or the exact construction of a ratio are not guaranteed
to match the book's specific definitions, and confirming that they do
would mean reading the library's source anyway. Every formula in this
project is kept traceable line-by-line back to the book rather than
delegated to a dependency whose internals are trusted on faith. Each metric
was checked by hand against a small synthetic wealth series before being
trusted on real data.

Among the five causal strategies, ONS is best on every metric reported --
higher Sharpe and Calmar ratios, lower maximum drawdown, and the most
statistically significant profitability ($p = 0.046$ against $p \approx
0.07$ for the rest). BAH, CRP, UP, and EG perform almost identically to
each other, which is visible directly in the wealth-curve plot
(Section~\ref{sec:viz}) as four lines that overlap for nearly the
entire window -- a genuine feature of how these four behave on this
particular basket over this particular year, not a rendering artifact.
Best Stock's numbers are far ahead on every metric, including
risk-adjusted ones, which is expected: it is choosing, with perfect
hindsight, the single asset that happened to perform best (Salesforce,
which nearly doubled over the window), while the causal strategies are
diversifying across a basket that also includes several laggards.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several gaps were identified during this work and deliberately left open
rather than addressed immediately, each for a stated reason:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No automated test suite.} Every component so far --
    benchmarks, Follow-the-Winner strategies, and the Chapter 2.3 metrics
    -- has been verified manually: by hand-computed checks on small
    synthetic examples, and by running the full pipeline against real
    data and inspecting the output. This was a deliberate choice to match
    the project's existing verification style rather than introduce a
    testing framework partway through, but it does not scale indefinitely
    as more algorithms are added.
    \item \textbf{Survivorship bias is mitigated, not eliminated.} The
    point-in-time Dow universe (Section~\ref{sec:survivorship}) fixes the
    ``chose today's winners'' mistake but not the underlying data
    limitation: \texttt{yfinance} cannot supply historical index
    membership through time or price history for delisted securities.
    A fully correct fix needs both, and both are capabilities the planned
    Bloomberg terminal migration is expected to provide -- the
    \texttt{get\_prices} indirection in \texttt{data.py} exists
    specifically so that migration touches one function, not every
    strategy or script.
    \item \textbf{A known but unfixed data bug.} \texttt{yfinance} returns
    multi-ticker downloads with columns sorted alphabetically rather than
    in the order tickers were requested. This does not affect any
    backtest result, since every strategy operates on asset columns
    positionally and consistently, but it would silently mislabel any
    future code that assumes the two orders match.
    \item \textbf{Remaining book chapters.} Follow the Loser, Pattern
    Matching, Meta-Algorithms, and the proposed learned strategy in this
    paper's own title are not yet implemented.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The value of this project so far is not only the six algorithms
implemented, but the practical realities that surfaced while translating
the book's idealized formulas into working code against real data: a
free data source's blind spot toward anything that did not survive to the
present, an architectural contract worth preserving even when a textbook
algorithm's own narration pulls against it, and formulas the book itself
states with inconsistent notation across chapters. Each of these changed
the scope of the work in a specific, traceable way. That process is
reported here as part of the project's contribution, not smoothed over in
favor of a cleaner-looking final result.
